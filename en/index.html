Camera died mid-recording? The footage is usually still there.

The camera shut off, the battery died, you pulled the card a second too early — and now there's a file no player will open. On Sony it shows up as an RSV file; on DJI it's an MP4 that says moov atom not found. It happens to everyone, and most of the time it can be saved. Here's what to know before you do anything.

What actually happened

The camera writes picture and sound to the card the whole time. But there's one more small part of the file — think of it as a map that tells a player where every frame sits — and the camera writes that map only at the end, when you press stop. If the power went before that, you're left with all the footage and no map, and no player knows where to start. That's why renaming the file to .mp4 doesn't help: it still has no map.

Before you do anything

  • Don't format the card.
  • Don't keep shooting on it. Whatever gets overwritten is gone.
  • Don't run a repair tool on the original file. Copy it first, work on the copy.
  • If the file reached you by transfer (WeTransfer, Drive) — check it arrived complete. A transfer that stalled halfway leaves a file that looks fine and is half empty inside, and no software repairs that.

How to repair it yourself

  1. A sample clip. You need a healthy recording from the same camera, at exactly the same settings: same resolution, same frames per second (25, 50, 100…), same recording quality. Length doesn't matter — put a card in, lens cap on, record 30 seconds.
  2. A free tool called untrunc. Download it here (Windows, with a simple interface). Pick the sample clip, pick the broken file, run it. It's exactly what the paid tools do. Sometimes it takes a second run.
  3. Then check. This is the part most people skip, and it's why they only find the problem once they sit down to edit.

Why a "repaired" file comes out jittery

It isn't random. All of these tools build the new map from the sample clip. If the sample doesn't match exactly, the map comes out slightly off. When you scrub in a player it looks fine — and then when you export, or open it in your editing app, the picture jumps, green blocks appear, or the sound drifts toward the end.

So before you trust a repaired file: check its duration matches what you actually shot. Scrub to five or six points across the file, especially near the end, and watch a few seconds at each. And make sure lips still match sound. If anything's off — a better-matched sample clip and another pass. Not a different program.
